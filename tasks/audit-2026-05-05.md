### Matched-solo-accuracy selection

This is the experimental control that lets us claim "LoRA and FT at
the *same* solo accuracy collaborate differently." We do not yet have
a script that:

1. Loads each `checkpoint-{1500,3000,4500,6000}` and the corresponding
   LoRA adapter.
2. Runs the *same* 50-mixed-MMLU 3-CoT-round solo protocol used by
   the LoRA pair-grid.
3. Reports which FT checkpoint matches the LoRA solo accuracy within
   a tolerance (±2 pp seems reasonable).
4. Picks that FT checkpoint as the comparison anchor.

Without this, any LoRA-vs-FT delta we report can be attributed to
"the FT specialist was better trained" (or worse trained). Building
this script is the highest-impact concrete next task.

### 6a. NEW finding from this audit: C2W:W2C count ratio is base-rate-confounded

Re-running the per-cell C2W/W2C numbers from
`matrix_results.json` (data already on disk; see
`/tmp/halulujah_audit/conditional_rates.json`):

| Setting | Pooled C2W count | Pooled W2C count | Pooled C2W:W2C count ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1.7B verified LoRA (5×5, 25 cells) | 82 | 625 | **0.13** |
| 4B Full FT (3 cells, +base helper) | 65 | 37 | **1.76** |

The raw count ratio at 1.7B LoRA is now *0.13* (W2C dominates), and
at 4B FT is *1.76* (C2W dominates). The earlier headline "3.4×
harmful switching invariant across protocols" (obj-007) was on the
PACE-pre-RP corpus where solo accuracy was high; with high solo,
there are many more "started correct" candidates than "started
wrong" candidates, so the count ratio is mechanically inflated.

When you normalize by the available pool (probability of switching
*conditioned on* starting correct vs starting wrong), the picture
flips:

| Setting | C2W given started-correct | W2C given started-wrong | Rate ratio | Status |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 1.7B verified LoRA (mean over 30 cells) | 32.7% | 46.7% | **0.70** | Exact (per_q recorded) |
| 4B Full FT (3 cells) | 14.7% | 23.3% | 0.63 | **Approximate** (denominator = solo accuracy) |

The 1.7B LoRA number is *exact* — the pair-grid runner records
`pre_a` and `pre_a_correct` per question, so we can compute the
denominator directly. The 4B FT number is *approximate* — the
existing 4B FT runner stores only the c2w/w2c counts, not per-question
pre_a, so we use solo accuracy as a denominator proxy.

**Bound analysis on the 4B FT rate ratio (audit follow-up #7).**
With pooled c2w=65, w2c=37, n=600, and pooled solo accuracy 73.5%:

| pre_a accuracy assumption | C2W&#124;C | W2C&#124;W | Rate ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Lower mathematical bound (pre = c2w/n = 0.108) | 100.0% | 6.9% | 14.5 |
| Conservative (pre = 0.5 × solo = 0.367) | ~30% | ~10% | ~3.0 |
| Central (pre = 0.7 × solo = 0.514) | ~21% | ~13% | ~1.6 |
| §6a current assumption (pre = solo = 0.735) | 14.7% | 23.3% | **0.63** |
| Upper mathematical bound (pre = 1 - w2c/n = 0.938) | 11.5% | 100.0% | 0.115 |

The §6a-as-written 0.63 number assumes pre_a accuracy *equals* solo
accuracy (n_rounds CoT). In the 1.7B grid, where we have ground truth,
pre_a accuracy is far *below* solo accuracy (cell-level pre_acc 2-44%
vs solo 12-38%) because pre_a is a 1-pass quick answer and solo runs
n_rounds CoT. If the same gap holds at 4B (pre_a ~70% of solo), the
true rate ratio is ~1.6 — i.e., **slightly C2W-asymmetric in the
harmful direction**, not net-helpful.

**This is the most fragile finding in the audit.** It does not
falsify the 1.7B number (which is exact), but it means §6a's
LoRA-vs-FT *direction* parity may not hold at 4B. The honest
phrasing is:

> *In the 1.7B verified-LoRA pair-grid, the conditional switch-rate
> ratio is 0.70 — collaboration is net-helpful in rank-normalized
> terms. In the 4B Full FT pair-grid the analogous ratio is between
> 0.6 and 1.6 depending on the pre_a-accuracy assumption; an
> exact value requires re-running the 4B FT collab with per_q
> capture (see audit follow-up #7).*

The 4B runner (`src/scripts/run_4b_full_ft.py`) has now been
patched (this commit) to capture `pre_a`, `pre_a_correct`,
`pre_a_accuracy`, `c2w_per_started_correct`, `w2c_per_started_wrong`,
and a full `per_q` list. The next 4B FT collab run will give the
exact rate ratio. Until then the §6a "both below 1.0" framing
should be hedged.

**Implication for the paper.** The headline "rank-constrained
adaptation destroys collaboration via C2W asymmetry" is not
defensible from the verified data when you control for the base-rate
artifact. The defensible claim is:

> *LoRA specialists update on peer answers more eagerly than
> Full-FT specialists at every solo-accuracy level we measured.
> The direction of switching (more flips toward correct than away,
> in both training regimes) is preserved by rank-constrained
> adaptation; only the magnitude is amplified.*

This is a smaller but cleaner claim. It is also consistent with
the intruder-dimension citation (Shuttleworth 2410.21228): rank
constraint introduces directions orthogonal to the pretrained
subspace, and those directions appear to amplify *all* updating,
not just harmful updating.

The audit recommends that **before any further paper writing**:

- The accuracy-delta claim and the C2W:W2C claim be presented as
  separate, with the latter explicitly framed as conditional rates
  rather than count ratios.
- The 1.7B FT pair-grid be run, so that the LoRA-vs-FT magnitude
  claim has a *paired* baseline rather than a 4B FT to 1.7B LoRA
  cross-comparison.
- The "matched solo accuracy" controls be enforced, since the rate
  ratios are a function of solo accuracy: math/medicine/physics LoRA
  are at 4-14% pre_acc on the pair-grid eval, while 4B FT bio/law/math
  are at 87/61/72%. These two settings cannot be compared directly.

## 6b. WHO-asymmetry ratio: canonical aggregator + roster sensitivity

### Reconciled to the obj-040 4.47× number

The 2026-05-04 confusion (4.47× obj-040 vs 4.74× audit-raw) was a
choice of cell value. obj-040's `plot_pair_grid.py` uses
**delta cells** (`pair_acc - solo_primary_acc`); my initial audit
recompute used **raw pair accuracy**. Both are mathematically valid;
they give different ratios because raw accuracy mixes the primary's
solo competence into the row spread.

**Canonical choice** (locked 2026-05-05 via
`src/scripts/compute_who_asymmetry.py`, summary at
`results/verified_pair_grid_qwen3_1p7b/who_summary.json`):

> Cell value = `pair_acc - solo_primary_acc` (delta).
> Roster = full 5×5 specialists, helpers = `[base] + DOMAINS` (6 cols).
> Row mean = mean across helpers; col mean = mean across primaries.
> Ratio = row_spread / col_spread.

Reasoning: the WHO-asymmetry claim is "primary identity affects
*gain from collaboration*". Subtracting solo_primary removes
row-wise baseline competence so the row spread reflects gain-vs-helpers,
not absolute task accuracy. With raw cells the row spread also
absorbs the solo-accuracy variance (e.g., biology 38% vs physics 12%
solo on this eval).

### Headline value: **4.47×**

Verified: `python -m src.scripts.compute_who_asymmetry --write` →
row spread 34.0 pp, col spread 7.6 pp, ratio 4.474. Exactly matches
obj-040's commit-message numbers.

### Roster-sensitivity sweep (canonical aggregator)

| Roster | Cell | Helpers | Row spread | Col spread | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Full 5×5 (canonical) | delta | + base | 34.0 pp | 7.6 pp | **4.47×** |
| Full 5×5 | delta | specialists only | 37.6 pp | 6.0 pp | 6.27× |
| 3×3 (math/bio/law)¹ | delta | + base | 32.5 pp | 10.0 pp | 3.25× |
| 3×3 (math/bio/law) | delta | specialists only | 35.3 pp | 10.0 pp | 3.53× |
| 4×4 minus law² | delta | + base | 30.5 pp | 10.0 pp | 3.05× |
| 4×4 minus law | delta | specialists only | 35.7 pp | 8.3 pp | 4.28× |

¹ Restricting to specialists where solo > base on the pair-grid eval.
² Excluding the law specialist that overfits CaseHOLD format.

**Range: 3.05× to 6.27×**, with the canonical headline at **4.47×**.

The most-deflating restrictions (4×4 minus law, 3×3) drop to ~3×;
removing the base helper (specialists only) inflates because the base
helper has the lowest col mean (17.2 pp) and removing it tightens the
helper distribution. The paper should:

1. Report **4.47×** as the canonical headline (full 5×5 + base helper, delta cells).
2. Cite the **3.05×–6.27×** range as the roster-sensitivity envelope in §4.
3. Note that the lower-bound 3.05× holds when the law CaseHOLD format-matcher is excluded.

(Earlier audit drafts proposed 3.85× as the conservative number under
the *raw-accuracy* aggregator on the 3×3 restricted roster. With the
canonical *delta* aggregator the same restriction gives 3.25×; this
is the new conservative lower bound to disclose.)

## 6a-bis. Per-primary conditional rate ratio: law is the lone outlier

Cell-level per-primary breakdown of the conditional rate ratio
(`/tmp/halulujah_audit/per_primary_conditional_rates.json`):

| Primary | Mean pre_acc | Mean C2W&#124;C | Mean W2C&#124;W | Rate ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| math | 3.7% | 36.1% | 42.9% | 0.84 |
| medicine | 10.0% | 26.8% | 49.1% | 0.55 |
| biology | 8.0% | 6.1% | 71.5% | **0.09** |
| law | 42.3% | 37.8% | 18.5% | **2.04** |
| physics | 10.0% | 56.5% | 51.5% | 1.10 |

| Pooled (cell-mean of cell ratios) | | |
|---|---:|---|
| All 5 primaries | 0.92 | (close to 1.0; no clear net direction) |
| Excluding law | **0.64** | (matches pooled 0.70 from §6a; net-helpful) |

**Law is the only primary with rate ratio > 1.0 in the entire grid.**
Every other specialist + base shows W2C|W > C2W|C — the agent flips
to correct more often than away from correct. Law inverts this:
C2W|C 37.8% vs W2C|W 18.5%.

This is consistent with §1's separate finding that the law specialist
is a CaseHOLD format-matcher: trained to recognise legal-citation
patterns (+24 pp on CaseHOLD-test) but lost +25/+13/+7 pp on three
MMLU-law subjects. When deliberating in MMLU's mixed format with
helpers from other domains, the law specialist does what every other
specialist does — *flip a lot* — but its training has shifted *which
direction* gets flipped. The pre_a accuracy of 42.3% (driven by
guessing patterns that happen to match MMLU-format incidentally)
is also far higher than any other primary's pre_acc.

**Implication.** The §6a "rate ratio 0.70 (net-helpful)" finding holds
for 4 of 5 LoRA specialists. Law is the entire reason the pooled
ratio is not even more strongly net-helpful. The conservative
publishable claim becomes:

> *Across the four 1.7B verified-LoRA specialists trained on cleaner
> (non-format-matching) distributions, the conditional switch-rate
> ratio is below 1.0 — switching is net-helpful in the rank-normalized
> sense. The law specialist (a CaseHOLD format-matcher) inverts this
> direction. The pooled 5-primary number 0.92 should not be cited
> without disclosing that one primary drives the asymmetry.*

This same "law is the outlier" pattern showed up in §6b
(WHO-asymmetry ratio drops from 4.47× full to 3.05× when law is
excluded). Two independent metrics converge on the same diagnosis.
The paper should:

1. Report 4 primaries (math, medicine, biology, physics) as the
   primary evidence, with law as a secondary case study.
2. Use law to *strengthen* the rank-amplification claim: "even when a
   LoRA specialist's training has been distorted by format-matching,
   the rank-amplification effect on switching rate is preserved
   (37.8% C2W|C vs the rank-mean of ~31%); only the *direction* of
   asymmetric flipping is changed by training-distribution quality."

## 6d. Switch-type breakdown including wrong→wrong (`other`)

The C2W:W2C framing has so far ignored a third switch type — `other`,
where pre_a is wrong AND post is wrong AND `pre_a != post` (the agent
flipped between two incorrect answers). Across all 1500 questions in
the 5×5 grid:

| Switch type | Count | Fraction |
|---|---:|---:|
| held (no switch) | 420 | 28.0% |
| c2w (correct → wrong) | 82 | 5.5% |
| w2c (wrong → correct) | 625 | 41.7% |
| **other (wrong → wrong)** | **373** | **24.9%** |

**One in four question-cells is a wrong→wrong noise switch.** The
rate-ratio framing in §6a treats these as a separate denominator
contribution (they're started-wrong, didn't recover), but they should
also be reported as evidence that LoRA specialists *churn* — they
flip rapidly even when no signal is available to flip toward.

Per-primary breakdown (`/tmp/halulujah_audit/switch_types_per_primary.json`):

| Primary | held | c2w | w2c | other | total switched |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| math | 33.3% | 2.0% | 41.3% | 23.3% | 66.7% |
| medicine | 17.7% | 3.3% | 44.3% | 34.7% | 82.3% |
| biology | 13.3% | 0.7% | 65.7% | 20.3% | 86.7% |
| law | 56.7% | 16.0% | 10.7% | 16.7% | 43.3% |
| physics | 19.0% | 5.3% | 46.3% | 29.3% | 81.0% |

Law again sticks out: its `held` rate is 57% (more than 2× any other
primary), and its `c2w` rate is 16% (more than 3× any other). Law
flips less often, but when it does flip, it's much more likely
to flip away from correct. Compare with biology, which flips 87% of
the time but flips correctly (W2C 66%) far more often than wrongly
(C2W 0.7% — *almost never*).

This refines the §6a-bis "law is the outlier" diagnosis. It's not just
that law has rate ratio > 1; law has a qualitatively different
*posture*: it commits to its initial answer, and when peer pressure
breaks that commitment, the broken-commitment direction is
asymmetrically harmful. Other LoRA specialists are more easily
swayed but tend to be swayed correctly.

### Recovery rates (P(post correct | pre wrong))

A cleaner way to read the row-by-row pattern:

| Primary | mean recovery rate (across helpers) |
|---|---:|
| math | 42.9% |
| medicine | 49.3% |
| biology | **71.4%** |
| law | **18.5%** |
| physics | 51.5% |

Biology recovers 7 out of 10 wrong starts; law recovers fewer than 2
out of 10. The verified-LoRA roster shows a 4× recovery-rate spread
across primaries with helper effect averaged out. Helper col-means on
recovery rate are tightly clumped 42-51% — once again, the primary
is the dominant axis.

## 6e. Helper-side breakdown — why base helper col mean is lowest

The col means in the canonical aggregator (§6b) range 17.2 → 24.8 pp.
The base helper sits at the floor (17.2 pp) and the highest-col-mean
helpers are math (24.8) and medicine/law (24.0 each). Why?

Per-helper averages across all 5 primaries
(`/tmp/halulujah_audit/per_helper_breakdown.json`):

| Helper | switch % | held % | C2W % | W2C % | other % | recovery | col mean delta |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| base | 69.2 | 30.8 | 5.6 | 38.4 | 25.2 | **44.7%** | 17.2 pp |
| math | 72.8 | 27.2 | 4.8 | 43.6 | 24.4 | 51.7% | 24.8 pp |
| medicine | 72.0 | 28.0 | 4.4 | 43.2 | 24.4 | 50.7% | 24.0 pp |
| biology | 71.6 | 28.4 | 6.4 | 40.0 | 25.2 | 47.4% | 19.6 pp |
| law | 77.2 | 22.8 | 6.0 | 45.2 | 26.0 | 52.8% | 24.0 pp |
| physics | 69.2 | 30.8 | 5.6 | 39.6 | 24.0 | 46.3% | 18.8 pp |

The pattern is consistent and physically intuitive:

- **Specialist helpers raise the primary's recovery rate by ~5 pp on
  average** (50% vs 45% for base). Even cross-domain specialists
  inject more useful signal than the base model does.
- **The col spread is mostly base-vs-specialist**, not specialist-
  vs-specialist. Within specialists, col-mean spread is only 6 pp
  (18.8 physics ↔ 24.8 math); base alone drops the floor by 7.6 pp.
- **Switch rates are tightly clustered (69-77%)** across all
  helpers. The primary's *willingness* to switch is roughly
  helper-invariant; what changes is *outcome quality*.

This sharpens the WHO-asymmetry interpretation. It is not "primary
matters and helper doesn't"; it's "primary matters a lot, *and*
specialist-vs-base helper matters a little (~5 pp), *and* helper
identity within specialists barely matters (~6 pp)". The two-stage
helper effect is small but coherent.

For the paper: this also disposes of the "Du et al. effect"
suspicion that the verified ratio inflates because we have an unfair
mix of weak (base) and strong (specialist) helpers. Even on the
**5×5 specialists-only** restriction (no base helper col), the
ratio rises to 6.27× — *higher*, not lower, because dropping the
base helper col tightens the helper distribution further.

| P(ratio > 3.0) | 67.1% |

**Direction is robust** — every bootstrap iteration gave a ratio > 1.
**Magnitude is wide** — the 95% CI spans 1.89 to 8.39, a more than
4× spread. The bootstrap mean (3.93) sits below the point estimate
because ratio distributions are right-skewed; the median 3.52 is
closer to the typical bootstrap value.

**Caveat resolved (2026-05-05, follow-up #9).** The audit-as-written
above said the underlying per_q records do not preserve question
identity across cells. That was incorrect — `run_verified_pair_grid.py`
loads its per-domain question pool *once* per primary (under a
deterministic seed=42) and reuses the same `test[primary]` list for
all 6 helper conditions. The per_q `idx` field is therefore a stable
join key on existing data; what was missing was an explicit `qid`.

`clustered_bootstrap_who.py` (committed in this audit-deepening pass)
exploits the implicit alignment to perform a question-clustered
bootstrap *without re-running the pair-grid*: it draws a single set
of question indices per primary and applies it across all 6 helper
columns of that primary, then recomputes the canonical aggregator.
Result (`results/verified_pair_grid_qwen3_1p7b/clustered_bootstrap.json`,
n=2000 iterations):

| Statistic | Within-cell (§6f as written) | Question-clustered (§6f revised) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Point estimate | 4.474 | 4.474 |
| Bootstrap mean | 3.93 | 4.59 |
| Bootstrap median | 3.52 | 4.36 |
| **95% CI** | **[1.89, 8.39]** | **[2.20, 7.45]** |
| 90% CI | [2.08, 6.99] | [2.59, 6.96] |
| P(ratio > 1) | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| P(ratio > 2) | 96.3% | 99.1% |
| P(ratio > 3) | 67.1% | 81.1% |

**Direction-of-prediction error.** The audit-as-written predicted
question-clustering would *widen* the CI (it shouldn't reduce
uncertainty). It did the opposite: clustered CI is **narrower**
(7.45 vs 8.39 upper) and the lower bound shifts *up* from 1.89 to
2.20. Both sides squeeze inward.

The reason is that the within-cell bootstrap injects independent
noise into all 30 cells, allowing cell-level deltas to drift in
arbitrary directions. The clustered bootstrap forces cells in the
same row to share question samples, which preserves the row-level
structure of "this primary is harder than that primary" and makes
the row-spread / col-spread ratio less responsive to within-cell
noise. The clustered CI is therefore the **correct** uncertainty
estimate; the within-cell CI was *too wide* on both sides, not too
narrow.

This is good news for the paper: the lower bound of the WHO-asymmetry
ratio is 2.20, comfortably above 1.0, with P(ratio > 2) = 99.1%.
The defensible round-number shorthand "approximately 4×" is
preserved; the lower-end "between 2× and 8×" envelope tightens to
"between 2.2× and 7.5×".

For the paper: report the headline as **WHO-asymmetry ratio = 4.47×
(95% CI 2.20–7.45, n=2000 question-clustered bootstrap iterations
on the verified 5×5 LoRA pair-grid)**. The within-cell bootstrap CI
[1.89, 8.39] should be reported in the appendix as a methodological
sanity check.

## 6g. Critical correction to §6a — X-parsing pollution

The §6a conditional rate ratio of 0.70 (and the per-primary §6a-bis
breakdown) used `pre_a_correct` as the rule for "started correct vs
started wrong." But `pre_a` is the *1-pass quick answer* extracted
from a fast prompt before any deliberation, and it includes a special
token `'X'` for parsing failures (output that the regex didn't
recognise as A/B/C/D). Across the 1500 question-cells in the verified
5×5 grid:

| Switch type | n | Of which `pre_a == 'X'` |
|---|---:|---:|
| C2W (correct → wrong) | 82 | 0 (impossible — X is never `pre_a_correct`) |
| W2C (wrong → correct) | 625 | **422 (67.5%)** |
| Other (wrong → wrong) | 373 | 210 (56.3%) |
| Held (no switch) | 420 | 142 (X→X, 33.8%) |
| **Total `pre_a == 'X'`** | | **774 / 1500 = 51.6%** |

**More than half of all `pre_a` records are parsing failures.** Of
the 625 W2C events that drove §6a's W2C|W rate of 46.7%, **422 are
"X → valid letter"** — i.e., the agent simply produced a letter
where it had previously failed parsing. These are *parsing recovery*
events, not behavioural updates from peer pressure. Removing them
from the W2C count and re-running:

| | C2W | W2C (letter only) | C2W rate (per started-correct) | W2C rate (per started-wrong-letter) | Rate ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| §6a as written (X-inclusive) | 82 | 625 | 32.7% | 46.7% | **0.70** |
| Letter-only (X excluded from numerator + denominator) | 82 | 203 | 36.9% | 40.3% | **0.92** |

The letter-only rate ratio is **0.92**, far closer to 1.0 than the
0.70 reported earlier. The "deliberation is net-helpful" headline
from §6a is mostly an artifact of pre-collaboration parsing failures
being counted as wrong.

### Per-primary letter-only rates

| Primary | pre_X | C2W | W2C from X | W2C from letter | Correct-pre | Wrong-letter-pre | C2W&#124;C | W2C-letter&#124;W-letter | **Letter-only ratio** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| math | 224 | 6 | 101 | 23 | 11 | 65 | 54.5% | 35.4% | **1.54** |

**Three of five primaries flip to ratio > 1.0 once X-parsing is

§6a "net-helpful (0.70)" framing.

Note also that the law specialist has **0% X at pre** — it is the
only roster specialist that consistently produces parseable letter
answers from a 1-pass prompt. This is consistent with §1's "law is a
CaseHOLD format-matcher" diagnosis: it has been trained to emit

### Implication for the paper

The §6a "conditional rate ratio = 0.70 (net-helpful)" claim is **not
defensible** as a headline. The defensible replacement:

> *Once parsing failures in the pre-collaboration answer extraction
> are excluded, the conditional switch rate ratio is 0.92 (pooled,
> N=725 letter-pre records), with substantial heterogeneity across
> primaries (0.13 biology, 0.64 medicine, 1.24 physics, 1.54 math,
> 2.04 law). The earlier 0.70 figure was inflated by 422 parsing-
> recovery events — agents that produced no parseable letter on the

> These are mechanically guaranteed to land in the W2C bucket and
> should not be interpreted as "deliberation correctly updated my
> answer."*

This is a substantial walk-back from §6a's framing. It does not
falsify the WHO-asymmetry ratio (which is computed on cell-level
*final* accuracies, not on switch types) but it does eliminate the
"both LoRA (0.70) and FT (0.63) are net-helpful" parity claim that
was queued for paper/claim_evidence_map.md C2/C5 in audit follow-up
#1.

The right next-step is **audit follow-up #10 (queued below):
re-run the verified pair-grid emitting a `pre_a_full` field that
captures the model's full pre-collab response (not just the
extracted letter), so the parsing artifact can be diagnosed

## 6i. Held-rate vs flip-rate scatter — rank-amplification refined

Per-cell Spearman correlations across the 30 (5 primary × 6 helper)

| Pair | Spearman ρ | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|

| held% vs W2C&#124;W% | **−0.92** | Stickiness *strongly* harms recovery |
| held% vs switch% | −1.00 (definitional) | (mechanical) |

This is the cleanest evidence yet for the rank-amplification

## 6j. Pair (X→Y) vs (Y→X) — the swap is highly asymmetric (delta version)

**Note on aggregation.** An earlier draft of this section used raw
pair accuracy (mean |swap| = 20pp). That comparison is not directly
interpretable as WHO-asymmetry because pair_X_Y uses X's
domain-specific question set and pair_Y_X uses Y's — the two cells
sit on different question sets. The legitimate single-pair
WHO-asymmetry comparison subtracts each primary's solo accuracy on
its own questions, isolating the gain-from-help (delta) on each
primary's domain.

For every off-diagonal cross-domain pair, compare delta_pair_X_Y =

|---|---|---:|---:|---:|

| medicine | biology | +22 | +36 | −14 |

**For the paper.** This is a strong figure-1 candidate. The

## 6l. Same-domain self-helper (pair_X_X) vs solo and vs +base

A clean control buried in the existing matrix: compare pair_X_X (the
specialist deliberating with a copy of itself) against pair_X_base
(the specialist with a base helper) and solo_X (the specialist alone).
If the specialist's contribution to deliberation comes from its
specialist knowledge, pair_X_X should beat pair_X_base. If
deliberation gain comes from "two voices in the room" regardless of
expertise, pair_X_X ≈ pair_X_base. If the specialist's voice is
*harmful* in a way that two copies amplify, pair_X_X < pair_X_base.

| Primary | solo | + base | + self | self − base |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| math | 0.360 | 0.520 | 0.520 | **+0.000** |
| medicine | 0.300 | 0.420 | 0.520 | +0.100 |
| biology | 0.380 | 0.680 | 0.680 | **+0.000** |
| law | 0.320 | 0.380 | 0.320 | **−0.060** |
| physics | 0.120 | 0.340 | 0.480 | +0.140 |

Three patterns:

1. **Talking to oneself ≈ talking to base** for math and biology.
   The specialists' marginal value as helper-to-self over base-as-
   helper is zero. Whatever deliberation gains they get, they get
   from base too.
2. **Self-help is harmful** for law (−6 pp). Law deliberating with
   law is *worse* than law deliberating with base. This is the third
   independent indicator (joining §6a-bis and §6c) that the law
   specialist is qualitatively different. Two CaseHOLD format-matchers

3. **Self-help substantially helps** for medicine and physics

phenomenon. Two of five specialists add nothing as helpers-to-self
over base; one is harmful; only two genuinely contribute. The
WHO-asymmetry mechanism is therefore *not* "primary expertise
boosts itself when echoed" — it's something more subtle, plausibly
"primary holds the question and drives outcome regardless of which
helper it talks to."

## 6m. Two-way variance decomposition — *primary explains 79–83% of pair-acc variance*

The §6b canonical aggregator uses row-spread (max−min) and
col-spread to compute the WHO-ratio. That captures *range*, not
*concentration*. A complementary measure is two-way variance
decomposition (effectively a two-way ANOVA without replicates,
treating residual = interaction + noise):

  SS_total   = Σ_{i,j} (cell_ij − grand_mean)²
  SS_primary = n_helper × Σ_i (row_mean_i − grand_mean)²
  SS_helper  = n_primary × Σ_j (col_mean_j − grand_mean)²
  SS_residual = SS_total − SS_primary − SS_helper

| Aggregator | SS_total | SS_primary | SS_helper | SS_residual | primary/helper |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Raw pair_acc | 0.5630 | 0.4464 (79.3%) | 0.0264 (4.7%) | 0.0902 (16.0%) | **16.93×** |
| Delta cells (canonical) | 0.6993 | 0.5827 (83.3%) | 0.0264 (3.8%) | 0.0902 (12.9%) | **22.11×** |

**Headline numbers:**

- Primary explains **79–83% of cell-level variance** in pair-grid
  outcomes (depending on raw vs delta aggregator).
- Helper explains **4–5%** of variance.
- Residual (interaction + noise) is **13–16%**.
- Primary/helper variance ratio is **16.9× (raw), 22.1× (delta)**.
- **95% CI on variance ratio (delta) = [5.78, 66.05]**, 2000-iteration
  question-clustered bootstrap. P(ratio > 1) = 100%; P(ratio > 5) = 98.9%.
  CI is much wider than the spread-ratio CI [2.20, 7.45] because the
  variance ratio is sensitive to the helper-side denominator (a small
  resampling-induced shift in helper col means rapidly changes
  SS_helper, blowing up the ratio).

This is a *much larger* WHO-asymmetry statement than the §6b
row-spread ratio of 4.47×. Both are valid; they measure different
things:

- **Row-spread ratio (4.47×, canonical headline)** captures the
  range of row-mean and col-mean, suppressing distributional shape.
  Sensitive to outliers, robust to magnitude.
- **Variance ratio (22.1× delta)** captures the proportion of total
  variance explained by primary vs helper. Sensitive to magnitude
  *and* shape.

**Reconciliation with the polluted-PACE 22.3×.** Obj-043's
question-clustered bootstrap on the polluted-PACE 10×9 corpus
reported asymmetry_ratio_median = 22.27 with primary_var_mean
3.2158 / helper_var_mean 0.1533 = 21×. That's a **variance ratio**,
not a spread ratio. The verified-roster equivalent is 22.1× (delta)
or 16.9× (raw). **The polluted-PACE 22.3× is no longer "5× too
large" — it's actually consistent with the verified data when you
use the same aggregator.** The previous §9 explanation of the
polluted-vs-verified gap conflated row-spread (4.47×) with the
variance-ratio (22.1×) the polluted analysis was actually computing.

This is good news for the audit trail: the polluted ratio was not
inflated by the empty-think bug; it was just measured with a
different (larger-magnitude) aggregator. The polluted bug invalidated
the *mechanistic interpretation* (deliberation didn't happen) but
not the row-vs-col variance comparison itself.

**For the paper.** Report both numbers under their proper names:

> *Two-way variance decomposition on the 30-cell verified
> Qwen3-1.7B LoRA pair-grid attributes 83.3% of delta-cell variance
> to primary identity, 3.8% to helper identity, and 12.9% to
> primary×helper interaction (residual). The primary/helper variance
> ratio is **22.1×**. The same data give a row-spread / col-spread
> ratio of 4.47× (95% CI 2.20–7.45 question-clustered bootstrap),
> which is a complementary range-based measure.*

The 22.1× is the *bigger* number for the headline; the 4.47× with
its CI is the more conservative spread-based measure to disclose
alongside.

Shannon entropy of the answer-letter distribution per cell, before
deliberation (`pre_a` letter, including X for parse-fail) vs after

| Primary | mean H_pre (bits) | mean H_post (bits) | mean ΔH |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| math | 1.36 | 2.10 | +0.74 |
| medicine | 1.50 | 2.27 | +0.77 |
| biology | 1.62 | 2.11 | +0.49 |

(Uniform 5-letter distribution = 2.32 bits; uniform 4-letter = 2.00 bits.)

**Deliberation does NOT collapse to consensus.** Across every

across {A, B, C, D, X}. Two mechanisms drive this:

1. **Parsing recovery (§6g).** Pre is heavy on X (51.6% of all pre

   The X "mass" disperses across A/B/C/D, raising entropy.
2. **Pair-induced disagreement.** Even within letter-only rows, the

   than its letter distribution before (consistent with the

   balanced between C2W and W2C).

its 0% pre-X rate and its low overall switch rate. Physics has the
largest (+0.92) — most parsing recovery, most diffusion. This
metric distinguishes the "format-matcher law" specialist from the
others by yet another channel.

**For the paper.** The entropy shift is a useful counter-intuitive
finding: the hypothesis "deliberation produces consensus" is
falsified on this verified roster. If consensus is what we want
from collaboration, LoRA specialists at this scale are not
producing it.

**The within-helper col_std (0.09–0.21) is 1.2× to 2.8× the

math (19.6 pp vs 24.8 pp col-mean)" is technically true but
uninformative — they overlap heavily.

**Reconciliation with §6m's variance decomposition.**

- SS_primary / SS_total = 83.3% (delta cells).
- SS_helper / SS_total = 3.8% (delta cells).
- SS_residual / SS_total = 12.9% (delta cells).

The §6m residual term *is* the within-helper-by-primary interaction.
12.9% of total variance is interaction, only 3.8% is helper main
effect — i.e. the helper's identity matters less than how that helper
interacts with a particular primary. §6p makes this concrete: it's
not that "math helper > biology helper", it's that "biology helper
shines on physics primaries (+48 pp) and underperforms on math
primaries (−6 pp) — and the directionality is reversed for math
helper."

**Implication for §6e's "specialist-vs-base helper matters a little
(~5 pp), helper identity within specialists barely matters (~6 pp)"
phrasing.** That phrasing collapses the interaction into the helper
main effect; the more honest version is "helper identity within
specialists barely matters *on average*, but specific helper-primary
pairings vary substantially". The paper's helper-side discussion
should retain the §6e col-mean line and add the §6p variance line:

> *Across 30 (primary × helper) cells, helper identity contributes
> 3.8% of total variance and primary × helper interaction
> contributes 12.9%. The helper main-effect col-mean spread of
> 7.6 pp is dwarfed by the within-helper across-primary spread
> (col_std 0.09 to 0.21). Helper-vs-helper comparisons are
> therefore meaningful only when conditioned on the primary; the
> "biology helper is worse than math helper" framing collapses
> when biology-helper-with-physics-primary (+48 pp) is contrasted
> with math-helper-with-physics-primary (+42 pp).*

This finally answers the §6e question "is the col-mean spread real?"
The col-mean *point estimates* are real (computed without sampling
noise on the underlying 50-question samples), but the *spread* of
those point estimates is small relative to the within-helper
variability. A reviewer who asked "would the helper col-mean ranking
hold under a different choice of primaries?" would get the answer
"no — it would shuffle, because the within-helper across-primary std
is larger than the across-helper col-mean spread."

## 6r. Replicate-aware 2-way ANOVA — interaction collapses, primary survives

The §6m two-way variance decomposition was computed *without
replicates* — each of the 30 (primary × helper) cells was a single
observation (the cell mean), so SS_residual = SS_total − SS_primary
− SS_helper conflated true primary × helper interaction with within-
cell question-level noise. Audit follow-up #13 (script:
`src/scripts/anova_replicates.py`, output:
`results/verified_pair_grid_qwen3_1p7b/anova_replicates.json`)
re-runs the decomposition on the (5 primary × 6 helper × 50 question)
1500-observation grid, using paired deltas
`Y_ijk = pair_correct[i,j,k] − solo_correct[i,k]` ∈ {−1, 0, +1}
(question alignment via the deterministic seed=42 idx — same
mechanism as §6f's clustered bootstrap).

### Decomposition

| Source | SS | frac total | df | MS | F | p |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Primary (A) | 29.14 | **6.6%** | 4 | 7.284 | **26.55** | **<1e-10** |
| Helper (B) | 1.32 | 0.3% | 5 | 0.264 | 0.96 | 0.44 |
| Interaction (A×B) | 4.51 | 1.0% | 20 | 0.226 | 0.82 | 0.69 |
| Within-cell (replicate) | 403.34 | **92.0%** | 1470 | 0.274 | — | — |
| Total | 438.31 | 100.0% | 1499 | — | — | — |

(The Y_ijk ∈ {−1, 0, +1} distribution is 93 negatives, 993 zeros, 414
positives — pair "improved on solo" 27.6% of the time, "regressed"
6.2%, "preserved" 66.2%.)

### What changes from §6m

The §6m headline was "primary explains 83.3% of variance, helper
3.8%, residual 12.9%" — *all on the 30-cell grid of cell means, with
no within-cell variance broken out*. The replicate-aware decomposition
shows where that variance actually lives:

| Component | §6m (cell means) | §6r (replicates) | Note |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Primary main | 83.3% | **6.6%** | Same effect, different normalization |
| Helper main | 3.8% | 0.3% | Same effect, different normalization |
| Residual / Interaction | 12.9% | 1.0% (interaction) + **92.0% (replicate noise)** | §6m's "residual" was mostly question-level noise |
| Within-cell | (not separable) | 92.0% | Newly visible in §6r |

The §6m "residual" term was overwhelmingly within-cell question-level
noise (q_i answered correctly with probability ~0.6 has variance
~0.24, consistent with the MS_within = 0.274 floor). True
primary × helper interaction is only **1.0% of total variance** and
**not statistically significant** (F(20, 1470) = 0.82, p = 0.69).

### The §6p prediction is half-confirmed

The §6p framing was: "within-helper col_std 9–21 pp ≫ between-helper
col-mean spread 7.6 pp predicts the interaction term will dominate
the helper main effect."

- **In SS terms:** SS_AB / SS_B = 4.51 / 1.32 = **3.42×** — YES,
  interaction's raw SS exceeds helper's. The §6p prediction holds
  on this axis.
- **In F-statistic terms:** F_AB / F_B = 0.82 / 0.96 = **0.86×** —
  NO, neither term is significant, and after dividing by their
  respective degrees of freedom (20 for interaction, 5 for helper)
  the mean squares are roughly equal.

The reason these go in opposite directions: SS scales with df, but
F-statistics divide by df. The interaction term has 20 df (5 × 4
contrasts) vs helper's 5 df, so a 3.4× SS difference becomes a
sub-1× F difference. Both terms are essentially at the within-cell
noise floor.

### Statistical significance

Only **the primary main effect** is significant on this decomposition:
F(4, 1470) = 26.55, p < 1e-10. Helper main (p = 0.44) and
interaction (p = 0.69) are *both not significant* once question-level
noise is accounted for as a separate variance component.

This is a substantially more honest reading of the data than §6m's
"22.1× variance ratio" framing. The 22.1× number is a comparison of
*cell-mean SS* between primary and helper rows; once you factor in
the within-cell variance, the helper main effect is statistically
indistinguishable from zero, and so is the interaction.

### Implication for the paper

The §10 abstract directive needs a third revision (already revised
once for §6q's subject stratification). The two-way ANOVA sentence
should now read:

> *Two-way ANOVA with question-level replicates (5 primary × 6
> helper × 50 question = 1500 observations) on the verified
> Qwen3-1.7B LoRA pair-grid attributes 6.6% of total variance to
> primary identity (F(4, 1470) = 26.55, p < 1e-10), 0.3% to helper
> identity (F = 0.96, p = 0.44), 1.0% to primary × helper interaction
> (F(20, 1470) = 0.82, p = 0.69), and 92.0% to within-cell
> question-level noise. Only the primary main effect is statistically
> significant. The §6m and §6q cell-mean variance ratios (22.1× and
> 20.0×) compare primary and helper SS at the cell-aggregate level;
> they should not be cited without the replicate-aware decomposition
> as a power audit.*

This **strengthens** the WHO-asymmetry headline (the primary effect
is highly significant — F = 26.55, p < 1e-10 is much stronger than
"4.47× point estimate with wide CI") while *deflating* the helper
and interaction claims. The paper's "primary identity dominates
collaboration outcome" thesis is now backed by a proper F-test, but
the corollary "helper identity has a small but real effect" should
be downgraded to "helper identity is not statistically distinguishable
from zero on this dataset" — which is the right caveat to include.

### Why the cell-mean and replicate-aware frames disagree

A quick reconciliation:

- The cell-mean SS_residual (12.9%) absorbs (a) true interaction
  + (b) within-cell noise pushed up to the cell level by 1/N
  averaging. With N=50 replicates, the within-cell-noise contribution
  to SS_residual is roughly SS_within / N = 403.34 / 50 = 8.07,
  and SS_AB at the cell-mean level is roughly SS_AB-replicate / N
  = 4.51 / 50 ≈ 0.09. So 12.9% breaks down as ≈ 12.0% from compressed
  noise + ≈ 0.9% from true interaction. The "interaction term" in
  §6m was 13× larger than the truth.
- The cell-mean SS_helper (3.8%) is real but underpowered relative
  to within-cell noise: F_B = 0.96 means the col-mean spread of 7.6 pp
  is not statistically distinguishable from what you'd expect if
  helper had no effect at all.
- The cell-mean SS_primary (83.3%) is also real and *highly*
  underpowered framing — the F-test gives p < 1e-10, but the
  cell-mean fraction "83.3%" is dominated by within-cell noise
  averaging into row means rather than by a bigger primary effect.

The cleanest paper claim: *Primary identity is highly significant
(F = 26.55, p < 1e-10) and explains 6.6% of question-level variance.
Helper identity and primary × helper interaction are not
statistically significant. Within-cell question-level noise is the
dominant variance component (92%), as is typical for binary
correctness data.*

## 6s. Per-question cross-helper agreement — helper matters at the question level but averages out

The §6r replicate-aware ANOVA put the helper main effect at 0.3% of
total variance (F = 0.96, p = 0.44 not significant). But that is a
*cell-mean* statement — averaged over 50 questions per helper, the
mean correctness was nearly identical. It does not say whether helper
identity affects which *individual* question gets answered correctly.

For each (primary, idx) tuple, we have 6 helper observations (the
final correctness booleans across the 6 helper columns of that
primary's row). Cross-helper agreement at the question level:

| Primary | Unanimous correctness¹ | Helpers disagree² | Unanimous letter³ | Mean across-helper var⁴ | Mean letter entropy (bits) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| math | 48.0% | 52.0% | 30.0% | 0.095 | 0.660 |
| medicine | 44.0% | **56.0%** | 30.0% | 0.107 | 0.715 |
| biology | **60.0%** | 40.0% | **54.0%** | 0.071 | 0.400 |
| law | 50.0% | 50.0% | 26.0% | 0.101 | **0.793** |
| physics | 52.0% | 48.0% | 30.0% | 0.094 | 0.690 |
| **pooled** | **50.8%** | **49.2%** | **34.0%** | **0.093** | **0.652** |

¹ Share of questions where all 6 helpers agree on right-or-wrong (regardless of letter).
² Share of questions where at least one helper differs from the others on correctness.
³ Share of questions where all 6 helpers produce the *same letter*.
⁴ Mean across questions of variance(correct) across the 6 helpers; max possible = 0.25.

Three readings:

1. **Helper *does* matter at the per-question level.** 49.2% of
   questions have helpers disagree on correctness — for roughly half
   the question pool, swapping the helper changes whether the
   primary gets it right. This is *not* compatible with "helper
   identity is irrelevant"; it *is* compatible with "helper identity
   matters at the question level but averages out across questions."
2. **Helper-induced disagreement is roughly bounded by question
   difficulty.** Mean across-helper var is 0.093 (out of max 0.25).
   Biology, the easiest primary (highest solo + highest pair
   accuracy), has the lowest cross-helper var (0.071) and the highest
   share of unanimous letters (54%) — easy questions converge across
   helpers. Law has the highest letter entropy (0.793 bits) — even
   when the 6 helpers agree on correctness, they hand the primary
   different *paths* to that correctness.
3. **The §6r helper insignificance is a cell-mean statement.** The
   helper main effect averaged over questions is small (0.3% of cell
   variance, F = 0.96, p = 0.44). The §6s per-question helper effect
   is large (49.2% of questions disagree across helpers). Both are
   true; the former is the right thing to cite for "is the helper
   effect statistically significant overall?" and the latter is the
   right thing to cite for "does helper choice matter for this
   particular question?"

For the paper:

> *At the cell-mean level, helper identity contributes 0.3% of
> variance and is not statistically significant (audit §6r). At the
> per-question level, helper identity flips correctness on 49.2% of
> questions (audit §6s) — close to the 50% one would observe under
> a coin-flip model. The two statements are reconciled by the
> "averaging out" interpretation: helper identity affects individual
> question outcomes but in directions that, pooled, do not produce
> a systematic helper-row main effect.*

This is consistent with the §6p observation that within-helper
across-primary col_std (9–21 pp) is large relative to the
between-helper col-mean spread (7.6 pp). Both findings say the same
thing in different normalizations: helper-identity-induced variance
is real but unstructured.

## 6t. Permutation test on the WHO variance ratio — p = 0.001 non-parametric

The §6r ANOVA gave parametric p < 1e-10 for the primary main effect
on the replicate-aware decomposition. The §6f bootstrap gave a 95% CI
[2.20, 7.45] for the spread ratio (canonical aggregator). A
non-parametric backup is a permutation test on the cell-mean
SS_primary / SS_helper ratio: shuffle the 30 cell values across the
5×6 grid (destroying any primary/helper structure), recompute the
ratio under each permutation, get a null distribution.

Script: `src/scripts/audit_6s_6t.py`. Output:
`results/verified_pair_grid_qwen3_1p7b/permutation_who.json`.

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---:|
| Observed cell-mean SS_rows / SS_cols | **22.11×** |
| Observed frac_rows | **83.3%** |
| Null median (5000 strong-shuffle iters) | 0.78 |
| Null 95% interval | [0.08, 6.10] |
| Null 99th percentile | 9.04 |
| Null max (5000 iters) | 49.95 |
| **p-value (P(null ratio ≥ observed))** | **0.0010** |
| p-value (P(null frac_rows ≥ observed)) | **<0.0001** |

5 of 5000 null permutations gave a ratio ≥ 22.11× (those reflect the
right-skewed tail visible from "max=49.95"). 0 of 5000 null
permutations gave a frac_rows ≥ 83.3%.

This is a non-parametric replication of the §6r ANOVA result with a
weaker test (5000 cell-mean permutations vs F-test with 1500 obs).
The two p-values disagree on order-of-magnitude (0.001 vs <1e-10)
because the parametric test exploits the replicate structure to get
much higher power; both reject the same null with overwhelming
strength.

For the paper, the permutation test is the right thing to cite when
a reviewer asks "is the parametric ANOVA assumption appropriate for
binary correctness data?" — the cell-mean ratio is itself a
distribution-free statistic, and the permutation null does not
depend on the underlying error distribution. The ANOVA is more
powerful when the assumptions hold; the permutation test is more
robust if they don't.

The two assumption-axes:

1. **Independence**: both tests assume independence of observations
   within a cell. The permutation test additionally assumes
   exchangeability of cell labels under the null.
2. **Homoscedasticity**: the parametric F-test assumes equal cell
   variances. The permutation test does not.

Neither test addresses **non-independence of cells within a primary
row** (the same 50 questions are used for all 6 helpers within a
primary, by deterministic seed=42). The §6f question-clustered
bootstrap accounts for this and gave a wider CI than the within-cell
bootstrap; the permutation test here is *strict-shuffle* (no
clustering) so the p-value is potentially anti-conservative. A
clustered-permutation-test version (shuffle whole rows or
within-row-only) would be a useful follow-up but is unlikely to
change the headline (the within-cell bootstrap already exceeded both
the question-clustered bootstrap and the permutation null).

## 6u. Cluster-respecting permutation test — primary effect carries the entire signal

The §6t strict-shuffle test reassigned all 30 cell values across the
5×6 grid, ignoring the within-primary question-clustering (the same
50 questions flow through all 6 helper conditions for a primary, by
deterministic seed=42). The cluster-respecting variant decomposes the
single combined p-value into a primary-only and helper-only test.

Script: `src/scripts/cluster_permutation_who.py`. Output:
`results/verified_pair_grid_qwen3_1p7b/permutation_who_clustered.json`.

### Two tests, two p-values

| Permutation type | Preserves | Tests | Observed ratio | Null median | p-value |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Strict shuffle (§6t) | nothing | combined | 22.11× | 0.78× | **0.0010** |
| **Within-row** (helpers exchangeable per primary) | SS_rows + Q-clustering | helper effect | 22.11× | 27.23× | **0.6468** |
| **Within-column** (primaries exchangeable per helper) | SS_cols (mixes Q-pools) | primary effect | 22.11× | 3.68× | **<0.0001** |

(All p-values from n=5000 permutations.)

### Key reconciliation

The within-row p-value of **0.6468** says: *under within-primary helper
exchangeability, the observed WHO ratio is typical of nulls* — the
helpers carry no structure beyond what random within-row rearrangement
would produce. The null *median* ratio under within-row shuffle is
27.23×, **higher** than observed (22.11×). Under this null, with five
rows of substantially different row-means (row spread 34.0 pp), even
a random rearrangement of helper labels within each row produces col
means with high apparent spread when the rows are sampled
unevenly — so the ratio under shuffle is wide and right-skewed.

The within-column p-value of **<0.0001** says: *under within-helper
primary exchangeability, the observed ratio is far higher than the
null distribution* — the primary effect is highly significant.

The §6t strict-shuffle p = 0.0010 is the *combined* test. The
decomposition shows it is *entirely* driven by the primary effect:
the helper component contributes p = 0.65, no signal.

### Triangulation with §6r ANOVA

The four statistical tests now in the audit converge on the same
qualitative answer:

| Test | Primary effect | Helper effect | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| §6f within-cell bootstrap | 95% CI [1.89, 8.39] (excludes 1) | (combined ratio) | §6f |
| §6f question-clustered bootstrap | 95% CI [2.20, 7.45] (excludes 1) | (combined ratio) | §6f revised |
| §6r replicate-aware ANOVA | F(4,1470) = 26.55, p < 1e-10 | F(5,1470) = 0.96, p = 0.44 | §6r |
| §6t strict-shuffle permutation | (combined p = 0.001) | (combined p = 0.001) | §6t |
| §6u within-column permutation | p < 0.0001 | — | §6u this section |

**Primary effect**: 4/4 tests reject H0 (no primary effect).
**Helper effect**: 2/2 tests fail to reject H0 (no helper effect).

The §6u within-row p-value (0.65) is the cleanest "helper effect is
absent" statement in the audit. It is robust to any normal-error
assumption (unlike F-tests) and respects the question-clustering
structure (unlike strict shuffle).

## 9. Verified vs polluted-PACE: ratio decomposition

For the audit trail, here is how the suspended 22.3× polluted-PACE
ratio compares to the verified numbers:

| Setting | Aggregator | Roster | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Polluted PACE 10-domain (obj-043, suspended) | question-clustered bootstrap on raw 1800-trial corpus | 10×9 | **22.3×** [10.2, 48.0] |
| Verified 1.7B LoRA 5×5 (obj-040, this audit canonical) | delta cells, mean-row-vs-mean-col | 5×6 | **4.47×** |
| Verified 5×5 LoRA — drop law | delta cells | 4×5 | 3.05× |

The polluted ratio is ~5× larger than the verified canonical. The
explanation is *not* a single confound; it's the conjunction of:

1. **Empty-think-tag pollution** (obj-042): the PACE pre-RP
   collaboration chains had no actual thinking content, so the
   "specialist's" answer was a single-pass MMLU letter. Inflates the
   row spread because primary-domain accuracy was a one-shot guess.
2. **MMLU-format pattern matching** (obj-024..028): PACE specialists
   were trained on MMLU and tested on MMLU; they learned the format,
   not the domain. After the verification gate (≥+5pp on ≥1 OOD
   benchmark), only specialists with *some* genuine domain transfer
   are accepted.
3. **Question-clustered bootstrap vs cell-mean variance**: bootstrap
   resamples *questions*, which inflates the apparent row spread when
   per-question variance is high. The cell-mean aggregator averages
   over questions, suppressing this.

Both diagnoses are real; both reduce the headline. The verified 4.47×
remains substantially above 1.0 and is the publishable figure.
